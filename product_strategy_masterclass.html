Market Analysis

Deep-dive into competitive landscape, customer needs, and untapped opportunities that define product-market fit.

Why Market Analysis Matters

Before Netflix pivoted from DVD rentals to streaming, they conducted exhaustive market analysis. They discovered that internet bandwidth would grow 10x in the next decade, that consumers preferred convenience over ownership, and that cable subscriptions were vulnerable to disruption. This single insight—backed by data—shaped a $250B company.
Core Truth: Products succeed not because they're technically brilliant, but because they solve a real problem better than alternatives. Market analysis reveals what that problem is and who's desperate to solve it.

The Three-Layer Analysis Framework

Layer 1: Customer Jobs

What are customers trying to accomplish? Not features—actual jobs. Instagram's job: "Help me feel connected to people I care about, instantly."

Layer 2: Competitive Landscape

Who else solves this job (directly or indirectly)? Airbnb competes with hotels, hostels, AND staying with friends. All are alternatives.

Layer 3: Underserved Segments

Which customers are frustrated with current solutions? Uber's insight: taxis were unreliable for regular people. That was the wedge.

Layer 4: Trends & Tailwinds

What macro trends create urgency? Remote work explosion = Figma's tailwind. Mobile ubiquity = Snapchat's tailwind. AI = your tailwind in 2026.

Slack's Market Analysis Insight
Slack's founder observed that enterprise communication tools (email, SMS, Jira) fragmented conversations across silos. Employees were drowning in notifications and context-switching. Slack's analysis: "The job is not chat—it's reducing cognitive overload and recreating water-cooler serendipity in remote teams." This insight meant Slack designed for channels, threading, and search—not just messaging. Result: $27B acquisition by Salesforce.

Quantifying Market Opportunity

A good market analysis includes numbers. Not guesses—real data.
TAM (Total Addressable Market): If every human in your target market bought your product, revenue = TAM. Example: LinkedIn TAM = all knowledge workers globally (~1.3B) × average enterprise software spend per worker. That's a $500B+ TAM.
SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market): How much can YOU capture with your go-to-market? If you're a bootstrapped startup targeting North America only, SAM ≠ TAM. It's smaller, realistic, achievable.
SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market): Your realistic Year 1 capture rate. Most startups aim for 1-3% of SAM in Year 1. Slack's Year 1 SOM ≈ $5M. They hit $15M. (Beat plan = good signal.)

Interactive: Validate Your Assumptions

You're building a SaaS product for freelance accountants. Which insight is most valuable for market analysis?
There are 2 million freelance accountants in the US
85% of freelance accountants spend 6+ hours/week on admin (invoicing, bookkeeping) instead of client work—and would pay $50/month to eliminate it
FreshBooks and Wave are competitors in the accounting software space

🎯 Key Takeaways: Market Analysis

  • Start with the job, not the feature. What is the customer really trying to accomplish?
  • Map your competitive landscape honestly. Direct competitors + indirect alternatives + "do nothing" all count.
  • Find the underserved segment. Uber didn't need every taxi rider—just enough frustrated ones to get started.
  • Quantify: TAM (theoretical) → SAM (regional) → SOM (Year 1 realistic). Investors want numbers backed by research.
  • Validate with data, not hunches. Survey customers. Talk to 50 potential users. Let their pain points guide your analysis.
  • Look for macro tailwinds. AI, remote work, mobile adoption—these make new markets viable. Ride the wave.
